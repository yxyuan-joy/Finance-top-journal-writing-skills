# Literature positioning

> SYNTHETIC SELF-USE OUTPUT: all manuscript facts and source names below were invented for this test.

Mandatory cybersecurity disclosures may affect liquidity policy even when their most visible consequences occur in capital markets. Existing work establishes several adjacent margins but does not yet connect them. Allen and Stone (2021) show how voluntary cyber-risk disclosure relates to stock-price reactions, while Baker, Chen, and Diaz (2022) develop a textual cyber-risk measure that predicts subsequent breaches. These studies make disclosure and measurement central objects, but the supplied evidence does not establish an effect on corporate cash holdings. A separate literature links supplier concentration to liquidity management: Clark and Iyer (2020) study supplier concentration and cash holdings without examining cybersecurity disclosure. Together, these findings motivate a joint question—whether firms with greater mandatory disclosure intensity also hold more cash, particularly when supplier relationships concentrate operating exposure—rather than the stronger claim that prior work has already identified a causal disclosure effect.

We study this question in a descriptive panel of U.S. public firms from 2011 through 2024. Disclosure intensity is associated with higher cash as a share of assets, and the association is larger among firms reporting concentrated suppliers. We interpret the latter pattern as exploratory evidence that the disclosure–cash relation varies with supplier risk, not as a mechanism test: the split was exploratory, and the design provides neither policy discontinuity nor other quasi-experimental variation. The paper therefore connects two previously separate empirical objects—cybersecurity disclosure exposure and supplier-linked liquidity management—while leaving the causal source of the association unresolved. Evans (2023) cannot yet define the remaining gap because only its title and journal metadata have been verified; any claim about its question or findings requires abstract or full-text review.

## Contribution claim

We document a joint disclosure–liquidity pattern by showing that cybersecurity disclosure intensity is associated with higher cash holdings, especially among firms with concentrated suppliers, while explicitly leaving causal and mechanism interpretations open.
