# Main-thread self-use output: intermediation and incidence

> SYNTHETIC SELF-USE OUTPUT: all manuscript facts below were invented for this test.

## Setting/design-to-results handoff

The fee cap changes one contract term at merchant cash-advance platforms, but its economic incidence cannot be inferred from the posted fee alone. Platforms can respond through approval, volume, and applicant selection, while merchants can apply more intensively or shift financing to other providers. We therefore compare merchants with greater versus lesser pre-rule exposure, measured from their contract mix before the cap, and read price and quantity responses jointly. This comparison measures differential changes by predetermined exposure; without further evidence on the exposure groups' counterfactual trends and concurrent changes, the estimates should not be described as the causal effect of the rule.

The post-rule patterns show lower offered fees but a contraction in access at the exposed platforms. Offered fees fall by 90 basis points, while approval probability falls by 5.2 percentage points and advance volume falls by 7.4%. Applications rise by 3.0%, so the decline in approvals cannot be read as weaker application demand. At the same time, the average risk score among approved merchants improves. That shift is consistent with tighter screening or a change in the applicant pool, but risk is not observed for rejected applicants; the data therefore cannot separate these explanations or recover the terms that rejected merchants would have received.

Observed substitution materially changes the interpretation of the platform-level contraction. Merchants replace 45% of lost platform funding through banks, implying that the 7.4% decline is a gross response at exposed platforms rather than a borrower-level net credit effect. Because financing from nonbank lenders outside the data is unobserved, even the remaining funding shortfall is not a market-wide estimate. Merchant sales are statistically inconclusive over the six-month horizon: the confidence interval permits outcomes ranging from a 2% decline to a 3% increase. The evidence thus documents lower offered fees, lower platform access and volume, and partial observed substitution; it does not establish a precise real effect.

The draft's three policy claims all exceed the evidence. “Expands affordable credit” conflicts with lower approvals and platform volume and ignores incomplete substitution. “Eliminates predatory lending” lacks a definition of predation and evidence on enforcement, avoidance, and lenders outside the data. “Improves merchant welfare” lacks realized borrower terms and the welfare accounting needed to compare the observed offered-fee decline with rationing and redistribution across merchants and providers.

## Bounded conclusion

The fee cap is followed by a 90-basis-point decline in offered fees at more-exposed platforms, together with a 5.2-percentage-point decline in approval probability and a 7.4% decline in advance volume. Rising applications and improved risk scores among approved merchants indicate that access and selection, not only posted prices, are central to the response, although missing risk for rejected applicants prevents a clean separation of screening from applicant composition. Banks replace 45% of lost platform funding, but unobserved nonbank finance prevents a market-wide net-credit estimate, and the six-month sales evidence is too imprecise to determine the real effect. The results therefore show a price–access tradeoff and partial substitution; they do not establish that the cap eliminates harmful lending or improves welfare.
