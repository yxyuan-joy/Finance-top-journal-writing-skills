## Revised quantitative-results and welfare section

### Quantitative discipline and model fit

We discipline the search-and-bargaining model using different sources for its central parameters. Dealer risk aversion is estimated from inventory-response moments, whereas investor search cost is calibrated from an external study. These inputs should not be described uniformly as identified by our data: the former is tied to variation in the estimation moments, while the latter imports discipline from outside the estimation sample.

The model targets five moments and closely matches four of them. Its principal targeted miss is trade-size dispersion, which it overpredicts by 25%. Because targeted moments enter the fitting exercise, their reproduction establishes in-sample fit rather than independent validation. An untargeted response provides a separate, and less favorable, diagnostic: following volatility shocks, the model underpredicts dealer exit by 40%. This miss matters for policy interpretation because the settlement experiment also changes dealer entry. The model can therefore quantify the experiment under its maintained entry mechanism, but the untargeted evidence cautions against treating the magnitude of that response as externally validated.

### Settlement counterfactual

We next use the fitted model to compare two-day settlement with a one-day settlement scenario. In the model, moving to T+1 raises trading volume by 6%, narrows spreads by 8 basis points, and reduces dealer entry by 5%. These are equilibrium counterfactual outputs, not reduced-form estimates of an observed reform. Moreover, T+1 lies outside the settlement-speed support of the estimation sample. The exercise therefore requires the model's bargaining, search, and entry relationships—and the invariance of the estimated and calibrated inputs—to extrapolate to the new regime. The 40% miss in the untargeted dealer-exit response is particularly relevant to the predicted entry effect.

### Welfare accounting and interpretation

Under the model's equal-weight welfare criterion, investor surplus rises by 1.3% and dealer surplus falls by 1.0%. The reported change in aggregate surplus is 0.2%, with a bootstrap 95% interval from −0.5% to 0.9%. Thus, even within the maintained model and welfare weights, the aggregate ranking is not statistically resolved: the interval includes both losses and gains. The manuscript should also define how the two group-specific percentage changes are aggregated into the reported total, rather than asking readers to add percentages with potentially different bases.

This welfare calculation is deliberately narrower than social welfare. It omits operational transition costs, default externalities, and endogenous investment in clearing technology. These omissions may change both the level and sign of the ranking, and the analysis compares T+1 with T+2 rather than optimizing over all feasible settlement arrangements. The supported conclusion is therefore conditional: the model predicts higher volume and narrower spreads under T+1, alongside lower dealer entry and redistribution from dealers toward investors, but it does not establish that T+1 is optimal or that it raises social welfare.
