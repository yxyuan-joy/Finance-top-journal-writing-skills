None of these measures is a sufficient statistic for “liquidity.” Together they indicate lower quoted and realized costs for small trades alongside worse depth, price impact, and large-order execution.

## Welfare and incidence boundary

The evidence does not support the claim that all traders benefit. The opposing execution-cost responses imply that the fee change redistributes trading costs across order sizes. Because trader identities are incomplete, we cannot map those order-size effects fully onto retail traders, institutions, or liquidity suppliers. Higher volume likewise does not establish a gain: it is an equilibrium quantity whose welfare meaning depends on why trading changes and on any external costs.

A welfare conclusion would additionally require an explicit objective, a counterfactual benchmark, and a treatment of relevant externalities and transfers. The analysis supplies none of these elements. In particular, a lower quoted spread cannot reveal whether gains to small orders outweigh losses to large orders, how fee revenue and liquidity-supplier incentives enter surplus, or whether changes in price impact create external costs. The unchanged price-efficiency diagnostic also provides no basis for asserting informational gains.

We therefore conclude that the fee change alters the composition of liquidity rather than unambiguously improving it. It lowers quoted spreads and small-order execution costs, but reduces depth and raises price impact and large-order costs. The resulting incidence and welfare ranking remain unresolved until trader groups and surplus components can be measured within a stated welfare framework.
