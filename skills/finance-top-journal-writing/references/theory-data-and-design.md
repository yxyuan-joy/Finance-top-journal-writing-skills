# Theory, Hypotheses, Setting, Data, and Design

## Theory and hypotheses

Use theory to generate discriminating logic, not decoration.

1. Define agents, choices, information, constraints, timing, and equilibrium object.
2. Explain the mechanism in words before or immediately after formal notation.
3. State which assumptions drive which predictions.
4. Separate directional predictions from ambiguous comparative statics.
5. Map each prediction to an observable test or explain why it is not directly testable.

Include formal hypotheses only when they improve discipline. Each hypothesis must identify the economic margin, direction or contrast, conditioning assumptions, and later test. Do not write post hoc hypotheses that simply restate known results.

## Institutional background

Include only rules and timing needed to understand measurement, assignment, behavior, or identification. Organize around the research design:

- actors and incentives;
- institutional sequence and dates;
- treatment/decision assignment;
- data-generating process;
- possible strategic response or spillover.

Conclude by linking the institution to the variation and key threats. Avoid a chronological history unrelated to the design.

## Data and sample

Make the sample reproducible:

1. Name sources and their roles.
2. Define unit of observation and period.
3. Describe linkage and deduplication.
4. Report inclusion/exclusion steps and resulting counts.
5. Define missing-data treatment and any winsorization/filtering.
6. Explain representativeness and selection.
7. State access, confidentiality, or vendor constraints when relevant.

Use a sample-flow table or appendix when construction has multiple gates. Reconcile every reported N across prose and exhibits.

## Variables and measurement

For each central construct, state:

- conceptual definition;
- operational measure and units;
- timing relative to treatment/outcome;
- aggregation level;
- known measurement error;
- validation or alternative measure;
- expected direction of bias when defensible.

Do not hide a constructed outcome behind a label. Explain why it represents the economic object.

## Empirical design

Move from question to estimand:

1. Define the target parameter or descriptive object.
2. Write the estimating equation only after defining its components.
3. Identify variation and comparison.
4. State the identifying assumptions in economic language.
5. Explain fixed effects, controls, weights, clustering, and inference by the threat they address.
6. Name threats the specification cannot solve.
7. Pre-map validation and robustness tests to those threats.

Do not say a design “controls for endogeneity.” Specify which source of confounding, selection, simultaneity, anticipation, measurement error, or interference it addresses.

## Methods and reproducibility

Report enough detail to understand the claim:

- preprocessing and estimation sample;
- model selection/tuning and train-validation-test separation when relevant;
- standard-error and dependence structure;
- multiple-testing correction when many outcomes/specifications are searched;
- preregistration or predetermined analysis when applicable;
- software/code/data availability in the appropriate end matter.

Move routine implementation detail to the appendix, but keep decisions that change interpretation in the main text.
